The National Security Case for Public AI

Jan 25, 2025 · Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator · A critique of the call for public AI

Introduction

The Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator position paper argues for publicly provided, owned, and operated layers in the AI stack (cloud, data, model development) and public utility–style regulation of the private AI industry. The critique below responds from the perspective of someone who has worked on both sides of government technology and supercomputing.

1. Framing and regulation flight

Paper: Public AI = (1) public layers in the stack; (2) public utility–style regulation fostering competition and preventing abuse. Altman’s framing: US/allied AI vs authoritarian AI.

Take: What stops innovation from moving to countries that don’t impose that regulation? If space, power, cooling, and money are the only constraints, several places outside the USA could be attractive. Regulation won’t work nearly as well when the workforce is remote and regulations aren’t globally aligned.

2. “Affordable mission-driven staff”

Paper

Investing in people with technological expertise could create a virtuous cycle: a more affordable mission-driven staff would build public-interested AI and evaluate private AI services better, reducing cost and quality problems on government contracts.

Take: One of the most nonsensical things I’ve read. Reads like someone who’s never worked on both sides. Likening AI leadership to healthcare.gov shows no understanding of how AI and its expertise differ from general IT. There is no “affordable mission-driven staff” in AI—are people at Meta and OpenAI “affordable” by taxpayer standards? The paper also says DOE will rely on private sector—so the public sector can’t stand on its own. The authors seem ignorant of open-source and effective public–private partnership. And the government can’t “invest” its human capacity problems away.

3. Coal/railroad and vertical integration

Paper: Structural separations between chip makers, cloud providers, and model developers (like coal vs railroads).

Take: Compare Intel (vertically integrated) and other chip vendors (not). A data center is not useful without GPUs, and a GPU isn’t useful without a model. The authors don’t actually know how the AI supply chain works.

4. Oligopoly, “innovative start-ups,” and geography

Paper: AI oligopoly will box out innovative start-ups, lose edge, offer worse service to government, raise costs. Nondiscrimination rules would level the playing field.

Take: At what level of the stack is “oligopoly” defined? What is an “innovative start-up” for multi-billion-dollar data centers or chipmakers relying on TSMC? Public utilities are geographically anchored; AI supply chain is global. Companies won’t lose edge from government contracts—they’ll lose it because other countries move faster. So GSA prices for everyone? Doesn’t antitrust cover this?

5. “Little competition” and government as innovator

Take: There’s an AI land grab and 60+ hour weeks—how is that “little competition”? Maybe there’s too much competition and the government needs to catch up. AI has no clear path to profitability yet—so how do we get monopolies unless government steps in? I’d love the long-form argument that AI would move faster with government involved. Mazzucato-style “government as engine of innovation” washes over the differences between AI and other tech.

6. Starlink, sole source, “free from conflicts”

Paper: Starlink example; contractor withholding; sole source = risk; public AI = “free from conflicts of interest.”

Take: How was Starlink “undermining” US policy? Citation needed for a major tech firm withholding over policy. Crowdstrike wasn’t sole source; not all airlines used it. Claiming government-made = “free from conflicts of interest” is absurd given campaign finance—authors are in an idealized world. “Improve ability to partner”—how? Magic wand?

7. DOE/NSF and national supercomputing

Take: Much of this report is a giant slap in the face to DOE and NSF supercomputing. They rely on contractors to deliver the closest thing to national AI infrastructure today. Absorbing them into federal government = more constrained, more compliance, less agility—out of touch with reality. DOE and NSF have shown the government doesn’t need to own everything. Decades of success through public–private partnership are ignored.

8. Cost overruns, thorny problems, and “both ways”

Paper: Cost overruns and delays standard; in-house would improve timing and budget; public AI for poverty, climate, disease without commercialization.

Take: Non sequitur—is it because of contracting? In-house = on-time and under-budget? DMV? Citation needed that contracting takes more time. Thorny problems are applications; most investment is in duplicating infrastructure, not apps. Companies do care—starving people = down profits. Private companies late/over budget “because the government lets them”? Show a case in leadership supercomputing. First-of-a-kind is hard and risky. Authors want both commodity pricing and risk-adjusted pricing.

9. Outsourcing and the expertise magic wand

Paper: Outsourcing saps knowledge and talent; in-house expertise would improve evaluation of contractors and fair pricing.

Take: Explain DOE ASCR and NNSA/SC given that. Can’t apply defense-sector findings when DOE/NSF supercomputing is the better analog. You can’t buy AI expertise at Walmart. Until government offers competitive compensation, compelling mission, and culture for high performers, there will be net egress of talent. Again—magic wand.

10. Big Tech, privacy, AI safety

Take: “Big Tech mixed record”—same for government; disingenuous to present as unique. Why only public AI with strict privacy? Should just regulate data privacy. AI safety “afterthought” → alternative firms and VCs funding them: that undercuts “can’t trust private industry.” “Large-scale destruction” isn’t what testimony said. Citing a podcast as authority undercuts credibility. Frontier models will be proprietary—need partnership. Vertically integrated government safety capability = not tractable. Build trust through partnership, don’t claim you’ll do it better with a wand.

11. “Open corporate environment” and release without testing

Paper: First to encounter superintelligence should be public sector; government in closed facility vs “open corporate environment”; corporate incentives = release without testing; government good at dangerous materials.

Take: There is no “open corporate environment” for frontier AI—facilities are at least as secure as classified. Nobody in industry has “release without testing” in the AGI business plan; superintelligence would be phenomenally expensive—not “person off the street.” Government’s “experience” is in domains where private sector isn’t allowed to compete—of course government has the track record.

12. Profit motive, complement, resilient, Congress

Paper: Profit motive doesn’t overlap with national interest; public AI would “complement” private; “resilient, uncompromised” capacity.

Take: Profit and national interest often align—stable nation needed for profits. Profit seeker could be president or Congress—weak argument. “Complement” = hard to distinguish from “compete against, poorly.” Resilient? Existing government HPC is unresilient; government far behind commercial. What is “uncompromised”? What happens when Congress can’t pass a budget? Contractors carried my salary; private sector doesn’t stand for idle datacenters.

13. Moon landing, hiring, one GPU / three cloud

Paper: Government as transformational innovator; hiring many new people with tech expertise is a feature; “one GPU manufacturer, three cloud providers, handful of labs” won’t provide what the US needs.

Take: Government innovates when there’s no commercial interest—moon, nukes. AI is profitable. Moon-landing analogy is a joke. Hiring = magic wand again; consensus = slow; who’d work in bureaucracy for less than industry pay (more than the president?); can’t buy expertise at Walmart. “One GPU manufacturer”—tell AMD; what about TSMC? Meta AI, Anthropic don’t fit “affiliated with cloud”; only OpenAI and Google do, and OpenAI is branching out. Intentionally hyperbolic.

Closing

The critique holds that the paper is unmoored from how the AI supply chain and talent markets work, from the existing success of DOE/NSF via contractors, and from the feasibility of a vertically integrated public AI (resilience, budget dysfunction, pay, culture). Bottom line: build trust with industry through partnership; don’t claim you’ll do what they do better, faster, and cheaper with a wave of the wand.
