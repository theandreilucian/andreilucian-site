1. A democratic vision for AI

Sam Altman’s op-ed speaks to government’s role in AI. The title: A democratic vision for artificial intelligence must prevail over an authoritarian one.

The piece is built around six pillars. No hype — policy, incentives, and infrastructure.

Quote

American AI firms and industry need robust security measures so the coalition keeps the lead and the private sector can innovate.

Quote

Government and the private sector should partner to develop those measures as fast as possible.

Quote

Public–private partnerships should build the infrastructure so U.S. firms have the compute to expand access and distribute AI’s benefits.

Quote

Coherent commercial diplomacy: clarity on export controls and foreign investment rules for the global build-out of AI.

Quote

Making open-sourced models available to developers in allied nations strengthens the coalition. Leadership in AI means exporting values, not just technology.

Quote

An investment fund for countries committed to democratic AI protocols could help them expand domestic compute.

Takeaway: Align incentives. Build infrastructure. Set rules. Keep the lead where it matters.

2. Azure SmartNICs

Azure Accelerated Networking runs on SmartNICs. First generation in every Azure compute server from 2015. The job: one NIC with full SR-IOV and GFT support.

GFT flows — VFP unified flows: L2/L3/L4 tuples and header transposition (HT) actions. Policy: VFP enforces on first packet of a flow; actions cached for the rest. Stateful flows → single core/thread. Pipeline: Parser → flow table lookup → Action (microcode; update without FPGA recompile).

Quote

Exception packet: FPGA overloads 802.1Q VLAN ID, forwards to hypervisor vPort; VFP performs flow creation.

Quote

Parser stage parses aggregated header information to determine encapsulation type.

Quote

Termination packets (SYN, RST, FIN): FPGA duplicates — one to destination, one to hypervisor vPort for VFP TCP state tracking and rule deletion.

Lazy flow updates: first packet of each flow as exception. Online serviceability: turn off hardware acceleration, fall back to synthetic vNICs. Transparent bonding: VF as slave, TCP/IP bound to synthetic NIC. RDMA serviceability transparency at the app level is still an open question.

Scalability at 100G/200G/400G: flow tables and actions in the NIC keep the data plane off the host.

3. Big Tech and clean power for AI

Big Tech Is Rushing to Find Clean Power to Fuel AI’s Insatiable Appetite — sustainability in HPC.

Quote

Tech companies are already the largest purchasers of wind and solar power.

Quote

Google pays a set rate that covers the difference between the cost of power and the lower-cost source the utility would have used. The utility can buy from a more expensive geothermal provider; Google covers the gap.

Quote

Tech companies and Nucor pay higher rates that help Duke Energy’s long-term costs and accelerate small nuclear and long-duration storage. Regulatory approval to fund such projects with ratepayer money alone is difficult.

Quote

Microsoft has struck deals with Occidental and Arbor (ex-SpaceX). It is by far the biggest buyer of carbon removal credits.

Takeaway: Demand for clean, firm power is real. Contracts that de-risk build-out matter.

4. Carbon removal — One dominant buyer

Carbon-Removal Firms Have One Very Big Backer. That’s a Problem.

Quote

Most of the money in carbon removal is coming from one buyer: Microsoft. Microsoft has made more than two-thirds of all carbon-removal purchases to date.

Patch, CUR8, CarbonX act as market makers between buyers and project developers.

Quote

Removal credits (pull CO₂ from the atmosphere) are higher quality than avoidance credits (prevent release). Much more expensive: avoidance under $20/ton; removal/DAC up to $1,100/ton. So a removal credit is on the order of 55× an avoidance credit.

One dominant buyer is a risk. Diversity of demand would make the market more resilient.

5. Datacenter emissions and genAI

Datacenters to emit 3× more carbon dioxide because of genAI.

Quote

Microsoft’s CO₂ emissions rose 29.1% from the 2020 baseline; much was indirect (Scope 3) from building more datacenters.

Quote

About 60% of that can be attributed to the power requirements of the facilities.

Reforestation and offsets are often discussed. The structural issue: more capacity, more power; the grid mix decides the footprint. Emissions reporting and power sourcing are no longer optional.

6. RDMA in LLM systems

Explorations of RDMA in LLM Systems. Collectives assume a fixed world; disaggregated inference (Prefillers, Decoders, KvCache) needs scale-up/down and tolerates failure.

Quote

Initializing the collective world is blocking; scale up or down and the whole world must pause.

Quote

Collectives guarantee global ordering; networks don’t. Some apps don’t want that — e.g. KvCache transfer, where only eventual arrival of all pages matters.

Quote

Collectives require same tensor shape and dtype; that can hurt ergonomics and performance (e.g. RPC with max message size).

Quote

Most RDMA uses RC (in-order). EFA uses SRD — reliable but unordered. SRD is datagram-based: send if you know the address. RC requires connection setup.

Quote

IBGDA lets GPUs initiate NIC ops directly — only ConnectX supports it; otherwise CPU mediation. CPU–GPU PCIe latency is only ~2 μs.

Takeaway: Collectives fit fixed training. For dynamic, disaggregated inference, you need something that doesn’t assume a fixed world. Custom inference engines and networking stacks are where the work is.

The DOE FASST RFI is part of the policy and infrastructure landscape. Responses (e.g. “FASST will be DOE’s opportunity to adapt, align, or…”) have been published separately; the RFI shapes how advanced computing and AI align with national priorities.
